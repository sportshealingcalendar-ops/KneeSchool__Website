Anterior cruciate ligament injury

Conditions library

A twisting injury to the ligament that stops the shin sliding forward. Common in pivoting sport, often missed at first, and rarely an emergency.

Review status. Submitted for consultant review on 14 September 2026. It carries no reference list yet, because references are attached at the verification stage and a missing reference is preferable to an invented one. Until a consultant has signed it off it must not be used to make a clinical decision.

Overview

One topic, seven depths. Choose the level you want and the same injury is explained again with more detail rather than different facts.

The anterior cruciate ligament runs diagonally through the middle of the knee and holds the shin bone back against the thigh bone. Most tears happen without contact, when a planted foot meets a sudden change of direction or an awkward landing.

The immediate picture is a pop, a rapid effusion and a knee that will not trust a turn. The longer term picture depends far more on what else was injured at the same time. The meniscus, the posteromedial corner and the cartilage surfaces determine how the knee does over decades, and the ligament itself is only the headline.

At a glance

  • Mechanism is usually non contact, on a planted foot.
  • Swelling within hours suggests blood in the joint.
  • Walking is often comfortable early, so tears get missed.
  • Reconstruction replaces the ligament with a graft.
  • Recovery runs to months, and rehabilitation drives the result.

The knee's strongest brake

The ACL is a short, tough band deep inside the knee. It runs diagonally from the thigh bone to the shin bone and stops the shin sliding forward. It also stops the knee twisting too far.

Most tears happen without anyone touching the player. A footballer plants a foot and turns, or a netballer lands awkwardly from a jump. There is often a pop, and the knee swells within a few hours. After that the knee can feel like it wants to give way when the person turns.

Training programmes that teach better landing and turning lower the risk, and they work best when a squad does them twice a week through the whole season rather than only in pre season. Girls and women tear the ACL more often than boys and men doing the same sport, and the reasons are still being studied.

If a knee pops, swells quickly or gives way, tell a parent, a teacher or a coach, and have it looked at by a health professional.

What it is and what a tear means for you

The anterior cruciate ligament is a strong cord inside the knee joint. Its main job is to stop the shin bone sliding forward on the thigh bone, and it helps control rotation when you turn.

A tear usually happens in a twisting movement rather than a collision. People often describe a pop, swelling within a few hours, and later a knee that feels unreliable when they change direction. Walking in a straight line is often comfortable quite early, which is why some tears are missed at first.

Not every tear needs an operation. The decision rests on how unstable the knee feels, what you need it to do, and whether anything else inside the joint was injured at the same time. Someone who plays pivoting sport and whose knee gives way is in a different position from someone whose knee is steady during daily activity.

Surgery replaces the torn ligament with a graft rather than stitching it back together, and the recovery is measured in months, not weeks. Rehabilitation matters as much as the operation, and it is the part you control.

Seek same day assessment for a knee that cannot take your weight after an injury, a knee that is hot and swollen with a fever, or new numbness or coldness in the foot.

Structure and function

The ACL is an intracapsular but extrasynovial ligament. It runs from the posteromedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle to the anterior intercondylar area of the tibia, passing forwards, medially and distally.

It carries two functional bundles, anteromedial and posterolateral, named for their tibial insertions. The anteromedial bundle tightens in flexion and the posterolateral bundle tightens closer to extension, so some part of the ligament is under tension through the arc of movement.

Function follows that arrangement. The ACL is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation, taking the large majority of the restraining force, and it acts as a secondary restraint to internal rotation and to varus and valgus angulation once the collaterals are compromised.

Blood supply comes mainly from the middle geniculate artery, with a synovial envelope that is richer proximally. Innervation from the posterior articular branch of the tibial nerve carries mechanoreceptors, which is the anatomical basis for the proprioceptive deficit seen after rupture.

Examination and imaging

Lachman is the most sensitive clinical test for a torn ACL and is performed at about twenty to thirty degrees of flexion. The anterior drawer at ninety degrees is less sensitive, particularly in the acute knee where hamstring guarding masks translation.

The pivot shift reproduces the rotatory instability that patients describe as giving way, and its grade corresponds more closely to symptoms than translation alone does. It is difficult to elicit in a painful acute knee and is often assessed under anaesthetic.

On plain radiographs look for a Segond fracture, an avulsion of the lateral tibial rim, which implies a cruciate rupture until proven otherwise. In a skeletally immature patient look for a tibial spine avulsion rather than a midsubstance tear. A deep lateral femoral notch sign is another indirect clue.

MRI confirms the tear and, more usefully, grades the associated injury: bone bruising in the lateral compartment, meniscal tears including ramp lesions of the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction, chondral damage and collateral or corner injury. Examine the whole knee on the scan, because the associated injuries change the operation.

Decision making and evidence

Reconstruct on instability and demand rather than on the scan. A patient whose knee gives way during the activities they need, and who has failed or declined a structured rehabilitation programme, is the clear indication. A stable knee in a patient with low pivoting demand is reasonably managed without surgery, with the caveat that recurrent instability episodes carry meniscal and chondral cost.

Graft choice trades re rupture risk against donor site morbidity and is age dependent. Hamstring autograft, bone patellar tendon bone autograft and quadriceps tendon autograft each have a defensible case. Allograft in a young pivoting athlete carries a materially higher failure rate and is difficult to justify outside revision or the older patient.

Timing is about the knee rather than the calendar. Operating on a stiff, swollen knee with poor quadriceps control increases the risk of arthrofibrosis, so restore range and extinguish the effusion first. The exception is a locked knee from a displaced bucket handle tear, which needs earlier surgery to save the meniscus.

Know the failure modes, because examiners ask for them: non anatomic tunnel placement, missed posterolateral corner injury, unaddressed ramp lesion or meniscal deficiency, excessive posterior tibial slope, and return to sport before criteria are met. Concomitant lateral extra articular tenodesis reduces graft failure in selected high risk patients.

Revision and the complex knee

Revision starts with why the first one failed. Take a history that separates a traumatic re injury from a knee that was never stable, and get long leg alignment views and a lateral to measure posterior tibial slope alongside CT for tunnel position and diameter.

Tunnel management decides whether this is one operation or two. Acceptably placed tunnels of modest diameter allow a single stage revision. Confluent, widened or badly malpositioned tunnels need bone grafting and a staged approach, with the interval dictated by graft incorporation rather than a fixed number of months.

Treat the knee, not the ligament. Meniscal deficiency, uncorrected coronal malalignment and an increased posterior tibial slope each load a revision graft, and leaving them is a common route to a second failure. Slope reducing anterior closing wedge osteotomy has a place in the revision knee with a markedly increased slope, although the threshold that justifies it is not settled.

Add a lateral extra articular procedure in the revision setting, in high grade pivot shift, and in young pivoting athletes returning to cutting sport. Counsel honestly: revision outcomes sit below primary outcomes for return to sport and for patient reported scores, and the patient should hear that before the operation rather than after it.

Where the debate sits

Repair has returned to the conversation for proximal avulsion tears with good tissue quality, supported by better patient selection and augmentation. Whether it holds up against reconstruction in young pivoting athletes over the medium term is an open question, and the early series are not yet long enough to settle it.

Slope is the argument that has moved furthest in the last decade. There is broad agreement that an increased posterior tibial slope loads the graft, and no agreement on the threshold at which correction becomes worth the additional operation, nor on whether it belongs in a primary reconstruction for a high risk patient.

Return to sport testing is widely used and weakly evidenced. Criterion based batteries are intuitively right and their effect on re rupture rates in adolescents is not established. Time based clearance alone is clearly inadequate, which is the part everyone agrees on.

Registry data has become good enough to shape graft choice at a population level while remaining a blunt instrument for the individual in front of you. The useful question for a department is whether its own outcomes match the registry, and whether anyone is looking.

Key learning points

What should survive if you remember nothing else from this page.

  • The ACL is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation and a secondary restraint to rotation.
  • The mechanism is usually non contact: a planted foot with a turn, or an awkward landing.
  • Lachman is the most sensitive clinical test. The pivot shift tracks symptoms more closely.
  • A Segond fracture implies a cruciate rupture until proven otherwise.
  • The decision to reconstruct rests on instability and demand, not on the scan alone.
  • Associated injury drives outcome: meniscus, ramp, corner, cartilage and slope all change the plan.

Imaging

The scan confirms the tear. Its real value is grading everything else.

Radiographs

Weight bearing views come first, and in an acute injury they are mostly a search for what the MRI might not lead with. A Segond fracture, an avulsion of the lateral tibial rim, implies a cruciate rupture. A tibial spine avulsion is the equivalent in a skeletally immature knee. A deep lateral femoral notch sign points the same way.

Alignment and slope

Long leg alignment views and a true lateral matter more than they used to, because coronal malalignment and an increased posterior tibial slope both load a graft. In a revision setting they are not optional.

MRI

Read the whole knee rather than the ligament. Bone bruising in the lateral compartment supports the mechanism. Meniscal tears, ramp lesions at the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction, chondral injury and collateral or corner damage each change the operation that follows.

What imaging does not decide

A torn ligament on a scan is not by itself an indication to operate. The examination, the instability the patient reports and the demands they place on the knee carry that decision.

Treatment

Two reasonable routes. The choice is about the person and their knee, not about the scan.

Without surgery

A structured rehabilitation programme restores range, quadriceps and hamstring strength and neuromuscular control. It suits a knee that stays stable through the activities the person actually needs. Recurrent giving way is the signal to reconsider, because each episode carries meniscal and chondral cost.

Reconstruction

A graft replaces the ligament. Hamstring, bone patellar tendon bone and quadriceps tendon autografts each have a defensible case, and the trade is re rupture risk against donor site morbidity. Allograft in a young pivoting athlete fails more often.

Timing

Operate on a quiet knee. Reconstructing a stiff, swollen knee with poor quadriceps control raises the risk of arthrofibrosis. The exception is a locked knee from a displaced bucket handle meniscal tear, where earlier surgery saves the meniscus.

What else needs doing

Repair the meniscus where it can be repaired. Address a ramp lesion, a posterolateral corner injury, malalignment and slope. A reconstruction that ignores the rest of the knee is a common route to a second one.

Rehabilitation

The operation takes an hour. The result is decided over the following year.

Early

Full extension and quadriceps activation come before anything else, because extension lost early is difficult to regain later. Swelling control and normal gait follow. Progress here is measured in what the knee can do, not in weeks elapsed.

Middle

Strength returns through progressive loading of the whole limb, including the hip and the calf. Single leg work exposes the deficits that double leg work hides.

Return to sport

Clearance rests on criteria rather than the calendar: symmetrical strength, hop test performance, movement quality under fatigue and confidence in the knee. Returning before those are met is associated with higher re rupture rates, particularly in adolescents.

The other knee

The uninjured side carries a meaningful risk of subsequent injury, so prevention work continues after the return rather than stopping at it.

Controversies

Where the evidence is thin or contested, this page says so rather than settling the question by tone.

  • Repair versus reconstruction in proximal avulsion tears with good tissue quality. Early series are promising and none are yet long enough to settle it.
  • The slope threshold at which a correcting osteotomy is worth the additional operation, and whether it belongs in a primary reconstruction for a high risk patient.
  • Lateral extra articular augmentation in primary reconstruction. Clear value in selected high risk patients, and an unsettled case for routine use.
  • Criterion based return to sport testing and whether it changes re rupture rates in adolescents, as opposed to changing when clinicians feel comfortable.
  • How registry data should shape graft choice in an individual, given that it describes populations well and individuals poorly.
